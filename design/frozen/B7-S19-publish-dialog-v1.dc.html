The publish dialog

The gate. Every validator result, then what the publish will actually change, then a name typed by hand — in that order, so nobody types before they read. It is the program's one absolute: a failing contrast pair stops the publish, and no state of this dialog contains a way past it. Not the blocked state, not an error, not the state where the validator itself is unavailable.

The design idea worth arguing about is the ordering. Validators sit above the blast summary because a person who reads “applies to 8 email templates and 2 widgets” first will scroll looking for the button, not the failures. Read the problems, then the reach, then commit.

Built on B1-DS v4. Two bilingual gaps and one fixed-token misuse were caught in review here, not by my own check — both corrections are named in the delivery note. Scheduled publishing is Doc 16 §19 and absent by design — §D says so where an operator would look for it.

Three failures and two warnings, grouped, each naming its fix and each opening the field that fixes it. S06 · light · en · the same absence, stated in both languages
S06 · the six states checked for an override, and the seventh
Section L listed six places an escape hatch could hide. This dialog adds the seventh and hardest: the validator service being unavailable. A publish gate that fails open the moment its checker is down is not a gate — so when contrast cannot be computed, publishing waits rather than proceeding. §C draws that state.
Note what this is not: it is not the breached-password check from Doc 02, which is explicitly allowed to fail open because it protects one account and blocking would lock a person out of their own reset. Contrast protects every reader of every surface, and its failure mode is that a brand ships unreadable. Different stakes, opposite defaults — and both are the document's own choices, not a house style.

Scheduled publishing is Doc 16 §19, out of v1. It is the single control an operator most expects on a gate like this — rebranding at midnight is exactly when you would want to queue it — so the footer states it rather than leaving someone hunting: publishing happens when you press the button, and the way to do it out of hours is to be there. Building it silently would have been the easy invention; leaving it silently absent would have been the quiet one.

The named restore point and the old/new-values half of the audit line existed only in English. §C now carries an Amharic published-state card with both, so all four claims — no “publish anyway” in any state, warnings publish and failures do not, the restore point is named, the publish is audited with old and new values — appear in each language. §A and §D remain English-only frames by design, as the English side of every pair.

Removing ceremony is not hiding an action. The bulk-bar law says an unavailable action stays visible with its reason, and this dialog honours it: the blocked header, the three named failures and their jump-to-fix buttons are all present and all explain themselves. The typed-name field is not an action — it is ceremony attached to an act that cannot proceed, and leaving it enabled invites someone to complete a ritual that ends in refusal. Actions keep their reasons; ceremony for an impossible act is removed.

Standing rule adopted, 3 August. Status hues — the eight pills, priority dots and SLA states — are reserved to their semantic components; generic pass/fail indicators use neutral success and danger tokens. This is the third recurrence of the same leak in this batch, so it graduates from note to law, and folds into the design system at its next legitimate version bump rather than reopening v4 for prose.

One correction, disclosed. The five validator pass glyphs rendered in #156F45 — B1-DS §2’s Open status text colour, carried forward by memory for a meaning that is not a ticket state. This is the third recurrence of that class in this batch, and the same one Section L corrected. They are now ink #1D1C18; pass and fail are carried by the glyph and the adjacent text, so nothing is lost. The lesson I am taking forward: read the token table for the label in front of me, never reuse a hex because it was correct somewhere else.

One judgement. The blocked dialog removes the typed-confirmation field rather than disabling it. A confirmation input on a dialog that cannot publish invites someone to fill it in and be refused, which teaches people to resent the gate. What sits there instead is the count of what to fix and a button that goes to the first one.
