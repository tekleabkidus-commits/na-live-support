Reset, invite, and setting a password

Two journeys into one screen. Someone who forgot a password and someone who has just been hired both end up typing a new one into the same form — and the platform must not tell either of them whether an account exists, which makes the first screen deliberately uninformative and the copy the hardest part of the section.

The set-password screen is where the policy stops being a settings row and becomes something a person reads while typing. Requirements are listed before they are broken, checked live, and the strength meter states its verdict in words — Doc 02 §13 forbids colour carrying that alone. This is also where A03 from Section A closes: the meter deferred from the login card ships here, where choosing a password actually happens.

Amharic is real text, flagged for native review. Components are B1-DS plus the five additions proposed in Sections A and B; the meter is proposed here as a sixth.

§A

Ask for a link, and the answer that tells you nothing

E01 · E02 · E12 · Doc 02 FR-16, SEC-01, AC-01, FS-02

The response is identical whether or not the address belongs to anyone, so the writing has to do the work an error message normally would: it names what was done, what to do next, and how long to wait, without ever confirming the account. “If an account exists” is the honest construction — and adding the spam-folder line and the resend timer means a real user is not left wondering whether the silence means they typed it wrong.

§B

Setting the password — policy you read, not policy you discover

E03 · E04 · E05 · E06 · A03 · Doc 02 FR-15, FR-16, §9, §13

Every requirement is on screen before the first keystroke and each one ticks as it is met, so nobody submits a password to find out what the rules were. The rules themselves are read from the tenant's settings — twelve characters here, five remembered passwords, breach checking on, character classes deliberately off — and the screen says which of them are the operator's choices rather than presenting them as laws of nature.

§C

The invite, and the links that no longer work

E07 · E08 · E09 · E10 · E11 · Doc 02 FR-17, EC-03, EC-04

A new agent's first screen is not a password field, it is a first impression — so the invite doubles as profile completion, and it says plainly that nobody chose this password for them. The dead-link screens matter just as much: a token that has expired or been used already is the most common way someone meets this product for the second time, and “request a new one” has to be right there rather than in an email they no longer have.

E11 · the link opened on a different device

Doc 02 EC-03 · allowed, then noticed

Requesting on a laptop and opening on a phone is normal behaviour, so nothing blocks it and there is no extra screen to design — the reset completes exactly as in §B. What happens instead is that the phone becomes a new device: the sign-in that follows fires the new-device notice from Section A (A14) and the new-device email from Section I. The row is delivered as that behaviour, not as a picture, because inventing a challenge screen here would contradict EC-03.

§E

Strength meter — proposed as B1-DS addition 06

closes A03 · Doc 02 §9, §13
06 · Password strength meter
Four segments, 34 × 5, radius 3, gap 4. Filled segments take the primary ink #22211D (dark: #ECEAE4), empty take the hairline #E5E2DB (dark: #45443E). The verdict is always spelled out beside it — weak, fair, good, strong — localized.
No red-amber-green. Doc 02 §13 requires strength conveyed as text rather than colour, so the meter deliberately has no hue at all — which also means it needs no new token.
A03 — now closed
Section A delivered the visibility toggle and left the meter open, because nobody chooses a password while signing in. It ships here, on the two screens where a password is actually chosen — reset (§B) and invite (§C) — in both themes and both languages. A03 can be marked done in the closing audit, with this file as its evidence.
