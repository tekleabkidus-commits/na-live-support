Branding — Sender identities

Three purposes, three from-addresses, and one status the operator cannot set. Verification is read from the DNS records infrastructure actually observes — so this screen reports a fact and never asserts one. There is no “mark as verified” control anywhere in it, because a sender an operator can declare good is a spoofing surface with extra steps.

The group's real subject is the lapse. DNS changes without warning, and when it does, customer email has to keep arriving — so the platform falls back to its own sender, keeps sending, and tells the operator plainly why their brand disappeared from the From line.

§B

Delivered: all five rows. P01, P02, P03 and P05 in §A at 1440 light with three states side by side — verified, waiting, and a domain that cannot be published from; P04 in §B at 1440 dark in Amharic with the English equivalent beneath it and the read-only 375 beside it.

Status arrives from Doc 18's DNS observation; the only control here re-runs the check, which asks infrastructure rather than telling it.

On the word “verified”, deliberately used here and nowhere else. This is a technical statement about a DNS record for a domain the operator controls; it says nothing about a person, and it must never rhyme with a customer-identity treatment — Doc 08 BR-01 and AC-09 prohibit any verified-person badge on customer surfaces, and nothing here is reused there.

Pill vocabulary corrected after review. A first pass dressed “Domain verified” in the ticket-status Resolved pair and “Lapsed” in the SLA-breached pair — pixel-identical to two fixed tokens, and the note defended the first while omitting the second. Sender state is a third vocabulary, like Section H’s account states, so it now borrows none of them: verified is the hairline presence pill with the #22A55B dot, lapsed is the generic error pair #FBE5E5/#8E2222, and waiting stays on the generic warning strip.
